Why the planner works the way it does
  • It asks when, not just what. People who wrote down a date and a time for a task followed through at meaningfully higher rates than people who just intended to do it; date alone was not enough (Milkman and colleagues, 2011; Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006).
  • Your progress bar never starts at zero. In a classic field test, people handed a card with two stamps already on it finished at 34 percent versus 19 percent for an empty card with the same real work left (Nunes and Dreze, 2006).
  • The five and seven day runs include one emergency skip. A hard goal with explicit slack built in beats both an easier goal and a rigid one, and a slip stops being a reason to quit (Sharif and Shu, 2017).
  • A missed day is never shown as failure. Research on streaks finds that showing people a broken streak suppresses the behavior itself, while repairable misses do not (Silverman and Barasch, 2023). One missed day also does not damage an emerging routine (Lally and colleagues, 2010).
  • Wins are recognized after you do the work, never dangled before it. Expected do-this-get-that rewards measurably undermine interest in the task itself; recognition of demonstrated skill strengthens it (Deci, Koestner and Ryan, 1999).
  • Every day after day one starts by retyping from memory. Pulling yesterday's query back out of your head is retrieval practice, the most reliable finding in learning science.
