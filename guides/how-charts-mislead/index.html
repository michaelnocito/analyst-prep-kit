How Charts Mislead: Eight Distortions, Worked on One Sample Table

By Michael Nocito, implementation analyst · Updated November 27, 2026

A chart makes its argument with shapes. Change the shapes and the message changes, even when every number stays the same. The table here is made-up sample data.

Same four numbers

Bars: East 3,040, South 2,670, North 2,495, West 1,685. East is 1.8 times West. The right chart makes it look like 16.9 times.

Check your last chart: Where does the value axis start? What time range does it show? Is any value drawn as an area?

The steps

1The cut-off axis1.8 times turns into 16.9›

East billed 3,040 and West billed 1,685. Check the real ratio with the practice data.

Type in an empty cell such as J1, or paste into the formula bar

=SUMIFS($H$2:$H$17,
   $D$2:$D$17,"East")
 /SUMIFS($H$2:$H$17,
   $D$2:$D$17,"West")

The result is about 1.80. A bar shows a value by its length. Start the axis above 0 and each bar shows only the amount above the start.

Nobody has to do this on purpose. Chart tools pick axis limits for you, so check the start.

Fix: start bar charts at 0. For lines, see should the axis start at zero.

2Percent or percentage pointsOne change, two very different numbers›

A conversion rate goes from 4% to 5%. It rose by 1 percentage point. It also rose by 25 percent. Both are true.

TermMeans38.6% to 40.6%
Percentage pointsNew rate minus old rate2 points
PercentChange divided by old rateabout 5.2%

Fix: say which one you mean, and show both rates next to each other. No axis or shape warns the reader about this one.

Quick check

A return rate falls from 10% to 8%. Which statement is right?

3The picked window and the missing monthStart dates and gaps change the story›

The practice data has orders in January, February, March and May. It has none in April.

  • February to March: up 15.1%.
  • January to February: down 14.6%.
  • January to May: down 7.6%.

A summary that returns only months with rows can draw January, February, March and May side by side. April disappears.

Fix: show the full period and say where it starts. Build the months from a calendar list, then match your data to it. Show 0 only when you know nothing was sold. If data did not load, mark the month as missing.

4Shares that hide the totalPercentages can rise while sales fall›
RegionRevenueShare
East3,04030.7%

Total9,890100%

Shares are rounded, so they may not add to exactly 100. A share chart cannot show whether the business grew. East could gain share while its revenue falls, if the others fall faster.

Fix: put the total on the chart, for example "Revenue by region, 9,890 total". To show growth, compare totals across periods.

Quick check

East’s share goes up. What can you say about East’s revenue?

5Pie chartsClose slices are hard to rank›

South is 27.0% and North is 25.2%. On a pie, that gap is about 6 degrees. On bars from 0, you compare two lengths against one scale, which people judge more accurately than angles.

  • A pie can work: two or three parts, where the point is "about half" or "about a quarter".
  • Use sorted bars: four or more parts, similar sizes, or comparing two periods.

More: choose the right chart.

Quick check

You need readers to rank six regions. Which chart?

6Area and 3DDouble the size, four times the ink›

You draw a picture twice as tall to mean "twice as much" and keep its proportions. The width doubles too, so the area is 4 times as large.

You doubleArea becomes
Bar height only2 times
Picture height and width4 times
Circle radius4 times

In a bubble chart, make the area match the value. If the radius matches the value, big values look far too big.

3D adds depth and tilt. In a tilted pie, front slices can look larger than equal slices at the back.

Fix: change one dimension only. Keep bar widths equal and charts flat.

Quick check

One value is double another. How much bigger should its bubble’s area be?

7Two value axesThe scales decide what looks related›

A dual-axis chart has one scale on the left and another on the right. Whoever sets those limits, you or the tool, changes whether the lines seem to move together, cross or split.

Safer choices:

  • Two charts stacked on the same time axis.
  • Index to 100: divide each value by the first value, then multiply by 100.
  • Chart the ratio, such as revenue per order.
MonthRevenue indexOrder count index
January100100
February85.4100
March98.3100
April00
May92.4100

Each month with orders has four of them, so order count stays at 100 while revenue moves.

If you keep two axes, label both and say so in the subtitle. Lines moving together do not prove one causes the other: see correlation vs causation.

Two lines on a dual-axis chart rise together. What does that prove?

8Log scalesHonest for wide ranges, easy to misread›

A log scale spaces values by ratio. Each tenfold rise takes the same height: 100 to 1,000 looks as tall as 1,000 to 10,000.

StepAddedTimes
100 to 3002003
300 to 1,000700about 3.3
1,000 to 3,0002,0003
3,000 to 10,0007,000about 3.3

On a log scale these four steps look almost equal. A steady growth rate draws a straight line.

  • Use it when values span several powers of ten, or percent change is the point.
  • It cannot show zero or negative values.
  • Fix: write "log scale" in the subtitle and label round values such as 100, 1,000 and 10,000.

0Should the axis start at zero?The rule depends on what shows the value›

Ask what carries the value. If it is the length or filled height of a shape, start at 0. If it is the position of a point or line, the axis may start elsewhere.

An example

Made-up on-time delivery rates for six months:

MonthOn time %
196.2
296.8
397.1
495.4
594.9
695.2

From month 3 to month 5 the rate fell 2.2 points. A line on a 0 to 100 axis looks almost flat. A line on a 94 to 98 axis shows the fall.

A non-zero line is not automatically safe. A 2020 study found that cutting the axis made differences feel bigger on line charts as well as bar charts, and axis break marks did not reliably fix it. Pick a range that matches how big a change matters, and state it.

Need both the level and the change? Draw two charts: levels as bars from 0, and the change on its own.

Set the limits in Excel (Windows)

  1. Click the chart. On the Format tab, open Chart Elements and choose Vertical (Value) Axis.
  2. Choose Format Selection.
  3. Under Axis Options, type the Minimum and Maximum.
  4. Set Major unit so gridlines land on easy numbers.
  5. Keep the axis labels on, and put the range in the subtitle.
Quick check

Which chart can fairly start above 0?

+Four more trapsOrder, color, small groups, precision›

Alphabetical order. If the question is ranking, sort by value. Keep natural orders such as months.

Color as the only signal. Among people of Northern European ancestry, about 1 in 12 males has red-green color vision deficiency. Add labels, position or a light and dark contrast.

Averages without counts. Show how many rows sit behind each bar.

RegionOrdersAverage order
East4760.0
South4667.5
West3561.7
North5499.0

West has the lowest total but not the lowest average. See mean vs median.

Extra decimals. Round to what the data can support.
